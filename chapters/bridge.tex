\chapter{The Bridge: from measurements to decisions}
\label{chap:bridge}

While the three repositories are independent, this short chapter shows how they work together end-to-end and how data flows between them.

\section{The pipeline}

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Measure} (JouleQuest): A testing campaign on a board generates raw data (CSV/JSON). We process this into a summary, and finally into an energy lookup table for that specific board.
    \item \textbf{Estimate} (JouleGrad): We load the lookup table into the \texttt{EnergyEstimator}. This tool provides differentiable energy estimates for the layer shapes we measured. By default, it refuses to guess for unmeasured shapes.
    \item \textbf{Search} (JouleNAS): The estimator is added to the training loss as $\lambda_E \widehat{E}$. The optimizer then prunes channels that cost more energy than they provide in accuracy, using the real measured costs of the target board.
\end{enumerate}

The key strength of this pipeline is that no numbers are guessed. The energy values driving the search come from real, physical measurements on the actual hardware, rather than theoretical FLOP counts or datasheet averages.

\section{From masks to physical deployment: validating with JouleQuest}
\label{sec:joulenas_to_joulequest}

So far, JouleNAS has evaluated pruned models by learning channel masks. While mask training is convenient and differentiable, it is still an emulation: the underlying PyTorch network still executes full-size dense convolutions and simply multiplies pruned channels by zero. To see real hardware energy savings and measure them on our NVIDIA Jetson board, we need to take the next step: \emph{deploying a physically smaller model}.

\subsection{Selecting the target models: \texorpdfstring{$\lambda_E = 0.005$}{lambda = 0.005} discrete}

To validate our methodology on physical hardware, we selected the CIFAR-10 ResNet-18 checkpoints obtained with $\lambda_E = 0.005$ in discrete mode for both benchmarked devices:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{NVIDIA Jetson AGX Orin}: Preserves $92.10\%$ test accuracy (compared to the $92.30\%$ dense baseline, a loss of only $0.20\%$) with a predicted energy fraction of $58.27\%$ (a $41.73\%$ energy saving).
    \item \textbf{Raspberry Pi 5}: Preserves $92.27\%$ test accuracy (compared to the $92.31\%$ dense baseline, losing only $0.04\%$) with an estimated energy fraction of $22.54\%$ (a $77.46\%$ predicted energy saving), pruning down to $60.40\%$ active channels.
\end{itemize}
Both checkpoints offer substantial predicted energy reductions well above physical measurement noise and environmental fluctuations.

\subsection{Reconstructing the hardware-specific pruned architectures}

In earlier iterations, pruned architectures were manually transcribed into static Python subclasses inside \texttt{layers/resnet.py} (such as \texttt{PrunedOrin\-Cifar\-ResNet18} and \texttt{PrunedPi5\-Cifar\-ResNet18}). While suitable for initial proofs-of-concept, this approach risked configuration drift between the controller, the remote measurement runner, and the comparison scripts.

To establish a single source of truth, we updated JouleQuest with a canonical, \textbf{spec-driven architecture pipeline} (\texttt{layers/pruned\_resnet.py}):
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Single source of truth}: The per-layer JouleNAS pruning JSON log serves as the sole input. The class \texttt{PrunedResNet18Spec.from\_pruning\_log} parses the log, validates tensor shapes, and propagates spatial resolutions.
    \item \textbf{Deterministic architecture identity}: From the validated configuration, the spec generates a canonical JSON representation and hashes it into a deterministic SHA-256 identifier (\texttt{architecture\_id}). This ID is embedded into measurement manifests, log files, and processed summary CSVs.
    \item \textbf{Cross-host deployment without filesystem coupling}: When launching remote experiments over SSH, the compact canonical JSON payload is passed directly via command-line arguments (\texttt{--pruned-config-json}), allowing the remote runner to instantiate the model dynamically (\texttt{PrunedResNet18\_32\_10.pt}) without requiring matching repository or log paths on controller and target boards.
    \item \textbf{Backwards compatibility}: We retain \texttt{materialize\_pruned\_model.py} strictly for offline inspection and verification (the former \texttt{--update-resnet} option was deprecated and removed), preserving historical named model classes as compatibility aliases.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Handling residual shortcuts and BatchNorm}

Mapping an irregularly pruned residual architecture onto hardware requires careful attention to topology and unmodelled operations:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Topological fidelity of shortcuts}: In standard ResNet architectures, identity shortcuts simply pass features forward when spatial stride is 1. If an internal residual block changes channel counts without an explicit downsampling layer, inserting a new $1 \times 1$ convolution would introduce operations that were never modelled during the JouleNAS search, distorting the energy comparison. In our spec-driven runner:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Learned $1 \times 1$ projection convolutions are instantiated \textbf{only} when a \texttt{downsample.0} layer is explicitly present in the pruning log (e.g., at stage downsampling transitions `layer2.0`, `layer3.0`, and `layer4.0`).
        \item For blocks with mismatched channel counts but no logged downsampler, the identity shortcut cleanly slices excess channels or appends zero-padding (\texttt{\_identity\_}\allowbreak\texttt{shortcut} in \texttt{layers/resnet.py}), ensuring exact structural fidelity with the search model.
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{BatchNorm and residual additions}: Each BatchNorm layer matches the active channels of its preceding convolution. While BatchNorm, activations (ReLU), pooling, and element-wise additions are not part of the isolated 21-term Conv/Linear lookup, their cumulative energy is explicitly isolated in the validation report.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Automated testing and validation in JouleQuest}

To evaluate pruned architectures systematically on physical hardware, JouleQuest provides the unified \texttt{pruned\_resnet18} suite in \texttt{run\_measurement\_campaign.sh}. Rather than relying on static class declarations or manual file edits, this suite takes any JouleNAS pruning JSON log as input and dynamically schedules a complete hardware verification campaign:
\begin{lstlisting}
PRUNING_LOG="measurements/pruning_logs/resnet18_..._pruning.json"

BACKEND=cuda NETWORK_BATCH_SIZE=32 \
./run_measurement_campaign.sh \
  --board agx_orin_bs32 \
  --suite pruned_resnet18 \
  --pruning-log "$PRUNING_LOG"
\end{lstlisting}
Adding \texttt{--dry-run} inspects the scheduled experiment matrix and verification plan without powering the board or executing inferences.

For each target architecture, the \texttt{pruned\_resnet18} suite automatically orchestrates:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item The unpruned dense baseline model (\texttt{ResNet18\_32\_10.pt});
    \item The complete materialized pruned model (\texttt{PrunedResNet18\_32\_10.pt}), built dynamically from the canonical specification;
    \item All constituent standalone \texttt{Conv2d} and \texttt{Linear} operations corresponding to the active shapes of the pruned network (e.g., 21 unique component shapes for our example log), all profiled at the specified batch size and linked to the same \texttt{architecture\_id}.
\end{enumerate}
(The earlier suites, \texttt{pruned\_}\allowbreak\texttt{validation\_}\allowbreak\texttt{orin} and \texttt{pruned\_}\allowbreak\texttt{validation\_}\allowbreak\texttt{pi5}, are preserved as legacy convenience aliases).

Physical testing on the Raspberry~Pi~5 revealed two key operational parameters for CPU-based embedded devices:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Inter-burst cooldown}: With a brief 3-second sleep, residual thermal buildup and dynamic CPU frequency scaling distorted dense baseline calibration. Extending the sleep period to 10~seconds allowed the CPU to cool completely between bursts.
    \item \textbf{CPU thread allocation}: Because the original layer lookup table on the Raspberry~Pi~5 was profiled using the full quad-core Cortex-A76 processor, we explicitly enforced 4 CPU threads (\texttt{CPU\_THREADS=4}) on the measurement runner to maintain strict hardware parity.
\end{itemize}
Under these stabilized, 4-thread conditions:
\begin{itemize}
    \item The dense ResNet-18 baseline consumed $28.38$\,mJ per inference (compared to the table-predicted $35.15$\,mJ, a $19.3\%$ composition gap).
    \item The materialized \texttt{PrunedPi5ResNet18} consumed $11.33$\,mJ per inference (predicted $7.93$\,mJ).
    \item Physical measurements showed a \textbf{60.08\% real-world energy reduction} on physical hardware (compared to the $77.46\%$ theoretical prediction), with only a negligible $0.04\%$ loss in test accuracy ($92.27\%$ vs $92.31\%$).
\end{itemize}

Once physical acquisition finishes and raw sensor traces are processed into summaries, the comparison tool \texttt{validate\_}\allowbreak\texttt{pruned\_}\allowbreak\texttt{measurements.py} evaluates the empirical results:
\begin{lstlisting}
python processing_report/validate_pruned_measurements.py \
  --board agx_orin_bs32 \
  --batch-size 32 \
  --summary-csv measurements/lookup_summaries/summaries/agx_orin_bs32.csv \
  --pruning-log "$PRUNING_LOG" \
  --composition-only
\end{lstlisting}
This tool formally decomposes and tests the composability equation:
\begin{equation}
    E_{\text{estimator}} = \sum_i n_i E_{\text{Conv2d}, i} + E_{\text{Linear}}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
    E_{\text{residual}} = E_{\text{whole}} - E_{\text{estimator}}
\end{equation}
Here, $E_{\text{residual}}$ explicitly isolates the physical energy attributable to BatchNorm, activations (ReLU), pooling, residual addition, memory transfers, CUDA launch costs, and system baseline power. Crucially, by separating $E_{\text{estimator}}$ from $E_{\text{residual}}$, the tool distinguishes between model composition fidelity and the physical overhead of unmodelled components, rather than conflating residual execution with measurement error.

\subsection{Physical hardware validation on NVIDIA Jetson AGX Orin (batch size 32)}
\label{sec:bridge_agxorin_bs32_validation}

\vspace{0.5em}

To close the loop on the embedded GPU target and explore batch scaling behavior on physical silicon, we deployed the materialized \texttt{PrunedOrinBs32ResNet18} (derived from the optimal $\lambda_E = 1.20$ checkpoint) alongside the dense baseline on the physical NVIDIA Jetson AGX Orin at batch size 32. While earlier testing used a temporary script (\texttt{validate\_agx\_orin\_bs32.sh}), we deprecated that dedicated wrapper in favor of the general campaign launcher:
\begin{lstlisting}
BACKEND=cuda NETWORK_BATCH_SIZE=32 \
./run_measurement_campaign.sh \
  --board agx_orin_bs32 \
  --suite pruned_resnet18 \
  --pruning-log "$PRUNING_LOG"
# or equivalently via the legacy compatibility alias:
NETWORK_BATCH_SIZE=32 ./run_measurement_campaign.sh \
  --board agx_orin_bs32 \
  --suite pruned_validation_orin_bs32
\end{lstlisting}

Physical power and energy traces recorded via the INA226 sensor yielded the following results:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Dense baseline fidelity}: The measured dense ResNet-18 consumed \textbf{$1.368$\,mJ per sample} (mean burst power: $8.07$\,W, burst duration: $16.8$\,ms). This matches the JouleNAS lookup table prediction of $1.372$\,mJ to within \textbf{$-0.3\%$} (a discrepancy of only $-0.004$\,mJ). This near-perfect alignment provides an encouraging hint to consider, suggesting that at batch size 32, launch overheads are amortized and isolated layer lookups compose much closer to the full architecture execution.
    \item \textbf{Materialized pruned model performance}: The physical model (\texttt{PrunedOrin\-Bs32\-ResNet18}) consumed \textbf{$1.095$\,mJ per sample} (mean burst power: $5.79$\,W, burst duration: $15.2$\,ms).
    \item \textbf{Real-world energy and power savings}: Physical measurements demonstrated a confirmed \textbf{$19.95\%$ real-world energy reduction} (saving $0.273$\,mJ per inference across all 32 samples) and a \textbf{$28.25\%$ reduction in average power dissipation} (dropping from $8.07$\,W to $5.79$\,W), while comfortably preserving $89.26\%$ classification accuracy (an accuracy delta of only $-2.60\%$).
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Analysis of the physical vs. predicted savings gap}
While JouleNAS predicted a theoretical energy reduction of $34.58\%$ ($0.898$\,mJ), physical hardware measurements yielded $19.95\%$ ($1.095$\,mJ), reflecting a composition discrepancy of $+22.0\%$ on the pruned architecture. Several hardware factors explain this residual difference on embedded GPU architectures:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Unaligned warp execution and Tensor Core tiling}: Deep structured pruning reduces channels to non-standard widths (such as $58, 127, 249, 236, 222$). Because modern NVIDIA Ampere architectures optimize GEMM routines for channel dimensions that are multiples of 32 or 64, irregular filter counts cause partial warp execution and lower compute efficiency per SM.
    \item \textbf{Residual additions, shortcut alignment, and BatchNorm}: While our spec-driven runner avoids inserting artificial $1 \times 1$ convolutions on identity shortcuts (using slicing and zero-padding), the physical network still executes real element-wise additions, BatchNorm, and pooling layers whose costs are part of $E_{\text{residual}}$ rather than the 21-term Conv2d/Linear lookup.
    \item \textbf{Static platform power floor}: Even during active computation, the Jetson AGX Orin baseline SoC components (memory controllers, CPU host interface, and PCIe peripherals) maintain a baseline idle power floor of $\approx 2.1$\,W, which dampens proportional energy reductions when execution time drops.
\end{enumerate}
Despite these low-level GPU realities, a physical energy reduction of $20\%$ accompanied by a $2.28$\,W drop in power dissipation provides an encouraging practical hint that JouleNAS can produce hardware-pruned networks that save substantial energy on real edge devices.

\section{Where the chain is weakest}

Two weak points need attention:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Composition}: We build the tables from isolated layer measurements, but the search uses them in full networks. This leads to a gap between the sum of the parts and the true total (Section \ref{sec:sum-of-parts}). The gap is small on Jetson boards but reaches up to about $25\%$ on the Raspberry~Pi~5.
    \item \textbf{Coverage}: The estimator only knows about the shapes we actually measured. If the search needs an unmeasured shape, the system refuses to guess by default. This is a deliberate safety feature so we don't trust unmeasured numbers.
\end{itemize}

Keeping these points in mind, the pipeline is ready to use and, as the next chapter explains, ready to be improved.
